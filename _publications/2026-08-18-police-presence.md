Government agencies are increasingly establishing an official presence on platforms whose value depends on user-generated content, yet little is known about how the arrival of an authority changes what users share. We study U.S. police departments joining Neighbors, a neighborhood safety platform, using a staggered difference-in-differences design on a multi-year zip-code panel. Police entry significantly reduces residents' voluntary contributions, and the decline is selective: it concentrates in safety posts about more uncertain, harder-to-justify observations, while more verifiable crime posts remain largely unaffected. A complementary survey experiment shows that perceived surveillance mediates the effect, supporting a self-censorship interpretation, and subsequent police engagement partially attenuates the chilling effect. The findings suggest that the key design question for platforms and agencies is not only whether officials should join online communities, but how they should engage once present.
